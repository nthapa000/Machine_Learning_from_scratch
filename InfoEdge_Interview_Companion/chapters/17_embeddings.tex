The syllabus says ``embeddings-based approaches (Word2Vec, FastText, BERT)''. The IIT Roorkee OA had questions on word embeddings;
the Tools slide lists BERT, Siamese networks and label attention networks; PremiumX retrieval uses a vector index. Embeddings are how
InfoEdge represents skills, titles, resumes and jobs so that ``similar'' becomes ``nearby''.

\section{From one-hot to dense vectors}
\textbf{One-hot}: a vocabulary of 50{,}000 words; each word is a 50{,}000-long vector with a single 1. Problems: huge, and every pair of
words is equally different (dot product 0): ``Python'' is as far from ``Django'' as from ``Accounting''.

\textbf{Embedding}: each word is a short dense vector (say 300 numbers) learned so that words used in similar contexts get similar vectors.
Similarity is measured by cosine. Dimensions are not individually named, but directions often encode meaning.

\begin{intuition}[: the distributional hypothesis]
``You shall know a word by the company it keeps'' (Firth). ``PyTorch'' and ``TensorFlow'' appear near the same words (``model'',
``training'', ``GPU'', ``deep learning''), so a method that predicts context words will give them similar vectors, even if they never
appear together.
\end{intuition}

\section{Word2Vec}
\subsection{Two training tasks}
Slide a window over text. For a centre word and the words within $\pm m$ positions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Skip-gram}: predict each context word from the centre word.
\item \textbf{CBOW} (continuous bag of words): predict the centre word from the average of its context vectors.
\end{itemize}
\begin{example}[: skip-gram training pairs]
Sentence ``senior data scientist python pune'' (5 words), window 2. Centre ``scientist'': pairs (scientist, senior), (scientist, data),
(scientist, python), (scientist, pune). Over all centres: $2 + 3 + 4 + 3 + 2 = 14$ pairs.
\end{example}

\subsection{The model}
Each word has two vectors: an input (centre) vector $\mathbf v_w$ and an output (context) vector $\mathbf u_w$. Score of context $c$ given centre $w$:
$\mathbf u_c^\top\mathbf v_w$; probability by softmax over the whole vocabulary:
\[ P(c\mid w) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf u_c^\top\mathbf v_w)}{\sum_{c'\in V}\exp(\mathbf u_{c'}^\top\mathbf v_w)}. \]
It is a neural network with one linear hidden layer (the embedding lookup, no activation) and a softmax output. The embedding table has
$|V|\times d$ parameters: 10{,}000 words $\times$ 300 $= 3$ million.

\subsection{Making it fast: negative sampling}
The softmax denominator sums over the whole vocabulary for every pair: far too slow. \textbf{Negative sampling} turns it into binary
classification: is $(w, c)$ a real pair (label 1) or a random fake pair (label 0)? For each real pair, draw $k$ (5--20) random ``negative''
words. Loss:
\[ -\log\sigma(\mathbf u_c^\top\mathbf v_w) - \sum_{n=1}^k\log\sigma(-\mathbf u_{n}^\top\mathbf v_w). \]
Each pair now costs $k + 1$ dot products instead of $|V|$. Negatives are drawn with probability $\propto f^{3/4}$ (frequency to the 3/4), which
gives rare words a bit more chance than raw frequency.
\begin{example}[: the 3/4 power]
Two words with counts 16 and 1: $16^{0.75} = 8$, $1^{0.75} = 1$: probabilities $8/9$ and $1/9$ instead of $16/17$ and $1/17$.
\end{example}
\textbf{Subsampling} frequent words: each occurrence of word $w$ is dropped with probability $1 - \sqrt{t/f(w)}$ ($t\approx10^{-5}$), so ``the''
does not flood training. For $f = 0.01$: $\sqrt{0.001} = 0.032$, so 97\% of occurrences are dropped.

\subsection{Famous property: analogies}
$\mathbf v_{king} - \mathbf v_{man} + \mathbf v_{woman}\approx\mathbf v_{queen}$. Relationships become directions. Toy 2-D: king $(3,4)$, man $(2,1)$,
woman $(2,3)$: result $(3, 6)$; the nearest word to it would be ``queen''. In job data: $\mathbf v_{\text{Java developer}} - \mathbf v_{\text{Java}} +
\mathbf v_{\text{Python}}\approx\mathbf v_{\text{Python developer}}$.

\subsection{Skip-gram vs CBOW}
Skip-gram: better for rare words and small data (each occurrence produces several training pairs); slower. CBOW: faster, smoother for frequent
words (averaging context).

\section{GloVe}
\textbf{Global Vectors} start from a word--word co-occurrence matrix $X$ (how often $j$ appears near $i$ in the whole corpus) and fit vectors so
that $\mathbf w_i^\top\tilde{\mathbf w}_j + b_i + \tilde b_j\approx\log X_{ij}$, with a weighting that down-weights rare and caps very frequent pairs.
It is a weighted matrix factorisation of log co-occurrences; Word2Vec does something similar implicitly (skip-gram with negative sampling
factorises a shifted PMI matrix).

\section{FastText: embeddings from character n-grams}
Represent each word as the sum of vectors of its character n-grams (3--6 characters, with boundary markers) plus the word itself.
``where'' with $n = 3$: \code{<wh, whe, her, ere, re>} (5 trigrams) plus \code{<where>}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{OOV words get vectors}: a typo ``pythn'' or a new skill ``langchainx'' shares n-grams with known words.
\item \textbf{Morphology}: ``developer'', ``development'', ``developing'' share n-grams.
\item Great for noisy user-generated text (resumes, queries) and morphologically rich languages (Hindi).
\end{itemize}
FastText is also a fast text classifier: average word/n-gram embeddings, then a linear softmax layer.

\section{Static vs contextual embeddings}
Word2Vec, GloVe and FastText give each word \emph{one} vector regardless of context: ``Java'' (language) and ``Java'' (island), ``bank'' (river vs
money), ``lead'' (team lead vs sales lead) get one blended vector. \textbf{Contextual} models produce a different vector for each occurrence,
computed from the whole sentence: ELMo (bidirectional LSTMs), then BERT (Transformers).

\section{BERT}
\subsection{Architecture}
A stack of Transformer \textbf{encoder} layers (Chapter 21). BERT-base: 12 layers, hidden size 768, 12 attention heads (64 dimensions each), about 110M
parameters; BERT-large: 24 layers, 1024 hidden, 16 heads, 340M. WordPiece vocabulary of about 30{,}000; maximum 512 tokens. Input embedding of each
token $=$ token embedding $+$ position embedding $+$ segment embedding (sentence A or B). Special tokens: \code{[CLS]} at the start (its final vector
summarises the sequence for classification), \code{[SEP]} between/after sentences, \code{[MASK]}.
\begin{example}[: BERT sizes]
Token embedding table: $30{,}522\times768 = 23.4$M parameters. Per-head dimension: $768/12 = 64$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Pre-training objectives}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Masked language modelling (MLM)}: choose 15\% of tokens; of those, replace 80\% with \code{[MASK]}, 10\% with a random token, keep 10\%
  unchanged; predict the original token. Because the model sees both left and right context, it is \textbf{bidirectional}. The 80/10/10 split exists
  because \code{[MASK]} never appears at fine-tuning time; mixing in random and unchanged tokens forces the model to build good representations for
  every token, not just masked ones. In a 512-token input, about 77 tokens are selected and about 61 actually replaced by \code{[MASK]}.
\item \textbf{Next sentence prediction (NSP)}: is sentence B the real next sentence? Later shown to be of little use (RoBERTa dropped it).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Fine-tuning}
Add a small task head on top and train the whole network briefly (2--4 epochs, learning rate $2$--$5\times10^{-5}$): \code{[CLS]} $\to$ linear layer
for classification (resume $\to$ job function); token vectors $\to$ per-token labels for NER (skill extraction); two segments for pair tasks (does
this CV match this JD?).

\subsection{The family}
RoBERTa (more data, longer training, dynamic masking, no NSP); DistilBERT (40\% smaller, 60\% faster, about 97\% of performance via distillation);
ALBERT (parameter sharing); domain models (a ``Naukri-BERT'' further pre-trained on resumes); multilingual BERT/IndicBERT. GPT models are decoder-only
and left-to-right (generation), BERT is encoder-only (understanding).

\section{Sentence and document embeddings}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Average of word vectors} (optionally TF-IDF weighted): simple, surprisingly strong baseline; loses order.
\item \textbf{BERT [CLS] or mean-pooled tokens}: off-the-shelf they are poor for similarity search.
\item \textbf{Sentence-BERT}: a \textbf{Siamese} (twin) BERT fine-tuned so that cosine similarity of the two pooled outputs matches semantic similarity
  (contrastive or triplet loss). The standard for semantic search and matching; this is the ``Siamese neural networks'' on the Tools slide.
\item \textbf{Bi-encoder vs cross-encoder}: bi-encoders embed query and document separately (fast, index millions, used for retrieval); a cross-encoder
  feeds both together through BERT (slow, more accurate, used to re-rank the top 100).
\end{itemize}

\begin{practical}[: embeddings at InfoEdge]
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Job--CV matching engine}: a two-tower (bi-encoder) model embeds jobs and resumes; ANN search retrieves candidates; a cross-encoder or
  GBM re-ranks.
\item \textbf{PremiumX hybrid retrieval}: vector index (dense) + keyword index (sparse), merged, then LLM-based ranking features.
\item \textbf{Taxonomy (SilverSkAI)}: FastText/BERT embeddings of 50 lakh raw skill strings, nearest-neighbour to 70K canonical skills.
\item \textbf{Label attention networks} (Tools slide): for multi-label tagging (which of thousands of skills a JD needs), each label gets its own attention
  over the text's token embeddings.
\end{itemize}
\end{practical}

\section{Interview questions}

\begin{qa}{\pyq{OA (IIT Roorkee): word embeddings} What are word embeddings and why are they better than one-hot vectors?}
\oneline{Dense, low-dimensional vectors learned from context so that words used similarly are close; unlike one-hot vectors they are compact, encode similarity,
and generalise from one word to its neighbours.}
\why One-hot: dimension $|V|$, all pairs orthogonal, nothing learned about ``Python'' helps ``Django''. Embeddings: 100--300 dimensions, cosine similarity
reflects meaning, and a model trained with ``Python'' examples transfers to related words. They are learned by predicting context (Word2Vec), from
co-occurrence statistics (GloVe), or inside a task model.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\pyq{OA (IIT Roorkee): CBOW vs skip-gram} Explain CBOW and skip-gram and when to use each.}
\oneline{CBOW predicts the centre word from its averaged context; skip-gram predicts each context word from the centre word; skip-gram is better for rare words
and smaller corpora, CBOW is faster and good for frequent words.}
\why Skip-gram generates $2m$ training pairs per position, giving rare words many updates; CBOW makes one prediction per position from an average, which
smooths over context noise.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\pyq{INT: BERT basics} Explain BERT: architecture, pre-training and fine-tuning.}
\oneline{BERT is a stack of Transformer encoders pre-trained on large text with masked language modelling (and originally next-sentence prediction), giving
bidirectional contextual token vectors; you fine-tune it end to end with a small task head.}
\why
\textbf{Architecture.} WordPiece tokens with token, position and segment embeddings; 12 (base) or 24 (large) encoder layers of multi-head self-attention and
feed-forward sublayers with residuals and LayerNorm.
\textbf{MLM.} 15\% of tokens selected; 80\% $\to$ [MASK], 10\% random, 10\% unchanged; predict the originals. Because attention is not masked, every token
sees left and right context: bidirectional.
\textbf{Fine-tuning.} [CLS] vector into a classifier; token vectors for tagging; sentence pairs for matching. Small learning rate, few epochs.
\pushes \emph{``Why not just train left-to-right?''} Many understanding tasks benefit from both sides of context (``the bank of the river''); MLM allows it
without the model trivially seeing the word it predicts. \emph{``BERT vs GPT?''} Encoder vs decoder; understanding vs generation; MLM vs next-token.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\pyq{INT: Word2Vec vs FastText vs BERT} Compare them.}
\oneline{Word2Vec learns one static vector per word from context prediction; FastText does the same but builds vectors from character n-grams, so it handles
misspellings and unseen words; BERT produces contextual vectors per token occurrence using a deep Transformer, so the same word gets different vectors in
different sentences.}
\why Cost and use: Word2Vec/FastText are tiny and fast (good features, similarity of skills); BERT is heavy but captures context (``lead'' in ``team lead'' vs
``sales lead''). For OOV: Word2Vec none, FastText yes, BERT via subwords.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{Why is the full softmax slow in Word2Vec and how does negative sampling fix it?}
\oneline{The softmax denominator sums over the entire vocabulary for every training pair; negative sampling replaces it with a binary task on the true pair and
$k$ random negatives, costing $k + 1$ dot products instead of $|V|$.}
\why With $|V| = 10^6$ and $k = 5$, that is a 160{,}000-fold reduction per pair. Hierarchical softmax (a binary tree, $\log_2|V|$ steps) is the other option.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{Count skip-gram pairs for a 5-word sentence with window 2, and FastText trigrams for ``where''.}
\oneline{14 pairs ($2 + 3 + 4 + 3 + 2$); 5 trigrams: \code{<wh, whe, her, ere, re>}.}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep Why does $\text{king} - \text{man} + \text{woman}\approx\text{queen}$ work?}
\oneline{Because the training objective makes vector differences encode consistent changes in context: words that differ only by gender co-occur with similar
words except for gendered ones, so their difference vectors align, and adding the gender direction to ``king'' lands near ``queen''.}
\why Formally, skip-gram with negative sampling approximately factorises a PMI matrix; log co-occurrence ratios that are constant across pairs (king:queen ::
man:woman) become constant vector offsets. Analogies are not perfect, and embeddings also encode social biases (``engineer'' closer to ``he''), which matters
for fair candidate ranking.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep Static embeddings give ``Java'' one vector. When does that hurt a job portal and what do you do?}
\oneline{When a word has several senses or the sense depends on context: ``Java'' (language vs coffee/island), ``lead'' (team lead vs sales lead), ``Spark''
(Apache Spark vs ``spark plug'' in automotive jobs); contextual embeddings (BERT) or phrase-level tokens and taxonomy disambiguation fix it.}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{Why does BERT mask with 80/10/10 rather than always using [MASK]?}
\oneline{Because [MASK] never appears in real fine-tuning text; replacing some selected tokens with random words or leaving them unchanged forces the model to keep
a good representation for every token and reduces the pre-train/fine-tune mismatch.}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{What are the dimensions of BERT-base, and how many parameters are in its token embedding table?}
\oneline{12 layers, hidden 768, 12 heads of 64, about 110M parameters; token embeddings $30{,}522\times768\approx23.4$M.}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep You need to search 50 lakh resumes semantically for each recruiter query in under 100 ms. Bi-encoder or cross-encoder?}
\oneline{A bi-encoder for retrieval, because resume vectors are pre-computed and indexed, so a query needs one encoding plus an ANN lookup; then a cross-encoder (or
GBM) to re-rank the top 100--200, where accuracy matters and the cost is affordable.}
\why A cross-encoder needs a full BERT pass per (query, resume) pair: 50 lakh passes per query is impossible. This retrieve-then-rank cascade is exactly the
Resdex ``retrieval + ranking'' design.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{What is a Siamese network, and how would you train one for job--CV matching?}
\oneline{Two identical encoders with shared weights map a job and a CV into the same vector space; trained with a contrastive, triplet or in-batch softmax loss so that
matched pairs (applied and shortlisted) are close and unmatched pairs are far.}
\why Positives: recruiter shortlisted the CV for the job. Negatives: random CVs (easy), CVs shown but not shortlisted (hard negatives). Triplet loss
$\max(0, d(a, p) - d(a, n) + \text{margin})$. In-batch negatives (every other job in the batch) make training efficient. Evaluate with recall@k.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{How would you build a sentence embedding from Word2Vec vectors, and what are its weaknesses?}
\oneline{Average the word vectors (optionally weighted by TF-IDF, removing the top principal component); it ignores word order and negation and is dominated by
frequent words, but is a fast, decent baseline.}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep How does FastText give a vector to a misspelled skill like ``kubernets''?}
\oneline{By summing the vectors of its character n-grams, most of which (``kub'', ``ube'', ``ber'', ``ern'', ``rne'') are shared with ``kubernetes'', so the
result lands near the correct word.}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{What are typical embedding dimensions, and how do you choose one?}
\oneline{100--300 for Word2Vec/FastText, 384--1024 for sentence Transformers; choose by downstream validation and the cost of storage and search (a vector index of
50 lakh $\times$ 768 floats is about 15 GB).}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{How do you evaluate embeddings?}
\oneline{Intrinsically (word similarity correlation with human scores, analogy accuracy, nearest-neighbour inspection) and extrinsically (performance of downstream
tasks such as retrieval recall@k or classification F1); extrinsic evaluation is what matters.}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep Would you fine-tune BERT or use it as a frozen feature extractor for classifying 2 lakh resumes into 40 job functions?}
\oneline{Fine-tune (or fine-tune a distilled version), because with 2 lakh labelled examples end-to-end training adapts representations to resume language and usually
gives clearly better accuracy; use frozen features only with very little data, tight compute, or many tasks sharing one embedding.}
\why Practical: DistilBERT or a domain-adapted model, max length 256--512 (or chunk), learning rate $3\times10^{-5}$, 2--3 epochs, class weights for rare functions,
macro F1 for evaluation. Compare against TF-IDF + linear SVM baseline.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{What did RoBERTa and DistilBERT change?}
\oneline{RoBERTa trained longer on more data with bigger batches, dynamic masking and no NSP, improving accuracy; DistilBERT used knowledge distillation to produce a
model 40\% smaller and 60\% faster with about 97\% of BERT's performance.}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{What is a label attention network (on InfoEdge's Tools slide), and why would InfoEdge use one?}
\oneline{A multi-label text classifier where each label has its own attention vector over the token representations, so each label looks at the parts of the text relevant
to it; useful for tagging job descriptions or resumes with many skills from a large taxonomy.}
\why A single pooled vector must serve thousands of labels; per-label attention lets ``Kubernetes'' focus on the DevOps paragraph and ``Negotiation'' on the sales paragraph,
and it gives interpretable highlights (``search highlights on the profile'' in PremiumX).
\end{qa}
