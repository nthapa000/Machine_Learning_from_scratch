\chapter{Gradient Descent and Optimisers}
\companion{StatQuest: Gradient Descent, Step-by-Step}{\ytid{sDv4f4s2SB8}}

Almost every model in this book is trained by some form of gradient descent: logistic regression, neural networks,
matrix factorisation, word embeddings, and (in function space) gradient boosting. InfoEdge has asked which is faster,
SGD or batch gradient descent (OA), what the learning rate is and how to adjust it, and what to do if training gets
stuck in a local optimum (interview).

\section{Walking downhill in fog}
You stand on a hillside in thick fog and want to reach the valley floor. You cannot see the valley, but you can feel
the slope under your feet. Strategy: step in the steepest downhill direction, a small step, then feel again. That is
gradient descent.

\begin{itemize}
\item The \textbf{landscape} is the loss $L(\mathbf w)$: height at each setting of the parameters.
\item The \textbf{slope} is the \textbf{gradient} $\nabla L(\mathbf w)$: a vector of partial derivatives, pointing in the
  direction of steepest \emph{ascent}. Its length says how steep.
\item The \textbf{step} goes the other way: $\mathbf w \leftarrow \mathbf w - \eta\,\nabla L(\mathbf w)$.
\item $\eta$ (eta) is the \textbf{learning rate}: how big a step relative to the slope.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What a derivative is, in one paragraph}
For a function of one number, $L(w)$, the derivative $L'(w)$ is the slope of the curve at $w$: how much $L$ changes per
tiny change in $w$. If $L'(w) > 0$, increasing $w$ increases $L$, so we should decrease $w$; if $L'(w) < 0$ we should
increase it. ``Move against the derivative'' always goes downhill. With many parameters, the partial derivative
$\partial L/\partial w_j$ is the slope along $w_j$ alone, and the gradient collects all of them.

\begin{example}[: three steps by hand]
$L(w) = (w-3)^2$, so $L'(w) = 2(w-3)$. Start $w_0 = 0$, $\eta = 0.1$.
Step 1: $L'(0) = -6$, $w_1 = 0 - 0.1\cdot(-6) = 0.6$.
Step 2: $L'(0.6) = -4.8$, $w_2 = 0.6 + 0.48 = 1.08$.
Step 3: $L'(1.08) = -3.84$, $w_3 = 1.464$.
Each step closes 20\% of the remaining gap to 3 ($w_{t+1} - 3 = 0.8(w_t - 3)$). Steps shrink automatically as the slope
flattens near the minimum.
\end{example}

\section{The learning rate}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Too small}: progress is painfully slow; training may stop (time budget) before converging.
\item \textbf{Too large}: steps overshoot the valley floor and land higher on the other side; the loss oscillates or
  blows up (NaN).
\item \textbf{Just right}: fast, steady decrease.
\end{itemize}

\begin{example}[: when it diverges]
Same $L = (w-3)^2$. The update is $w_{t+1} - 3 = (1 - 2\eta)(w_t - 3)$. This shrinks the gap only if
$|1 - 2\eta| < 1$, i.e. $0 < \eta < 1$. At $\eta = 0.5$ it jumps to 3 in one step. At $\eta = 0.9$ it oscillates
($1 - 1.8 = -0.8$) but converges. At $\eta = 1.1$, the factor is $-1.2$: the gap grows by 20\% each step, with alternating
sign. In general, for a quadratic with curvature (second derivative) $h$, GD converges iff $\eta < 2/h$.
\end{example}

With several parameters, the steepest direction can have curvature 50 and the flattest 1; $\eta$ must be below $2/50$
for stability, which makes progress along the flat direction 50 times slower. This ratio is the \textbf{condition
number}, and it is why \textbf{feature scaling} matters: unscaled features (salary in rupees next to experience in years)
create long, narrow valleys where GD zig-zags.

\subsection{Schedules}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Step decay}: divide $\eta$ by 10 every few epochs.
\item \textbf{Exponential decay}: $\eta_t = \eta_0\gamma^t$.
\item \textbf{Cosine annealing}: $\eta_t = \eta_{min} + \frac12(\eta_{max} - \eta_{min})(1 + \cos(\pi t/T))$.
\item \textbf{Warmup}: start tiny and ramp up over the first few hundred steps (standard for Transformers, where early
  gradients are unreliable and Adam's statistics are not yet warmed up).
\item \textbf{Reduce on plateau}: cut $\eta$ when validation loss stops improving.
\item \textbf{LR range test}: increase $\eta$ exponentially over one short run, plot loss, choose a value a bit below where
  loss is falling fastest.
\end{itemize}

\section{Batch, stochastic and mini-batch}
The loss is an average over $n$ examples, so its gradient is the average of $n$ per-example gradients.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Batch GD}: use all $n$ examples per step. Exact gradient, smooth path, but one step costs a full pass over
  the data. With 10 million rows, that is very slow per step.
\item \textbf{Stochastic GD (SGD)}: use one random example per step. Very cheap, very noisy steps.
\item \textbf{Mini-batch GD}: use a batch of $B$ examples (32--1024). The standard in practice: noise is reduced by
  averaging, and GPUs process a batch in parallel almost as fast as one example.
\end{itemize}
An \textbf{epoch} is one pass through the data. With $n = 50{,}000$ and $B = 250$, one epoch is 200 updates.

\begin{intuition}[: why noisy steps can be good]
A random sample of examples gives an unbiased but noisy estimate of the true gradient (on average correct). The noise
jostles the parameters, which helps escape saddle points and shallow local minima and tends to settle in wider valleys,
which generalise better. The price: SGD with a fixed learning rate never settles exactly; it jitters around the minimum
in a cloud whose size is proportional to $\eta$. Decaying $\eta$ shrinks the cloud.
\end{intuition}

\section{Convexity, local minima and saddle points}
\begin{itemize}
\item A function is \textbf{convex} if the straight line between any two points on its graph lies above the graph: a
  bowl. Any local minimum is global. Linear regression (MSE), logistic regression (log loss), SVM (hinge + L2) and Lasso
  are convex.
\item Neural network losses are \textbf{non-convex}: many minima and many \textbf{saddle points} (flat points that go up in
  some directions and down in others, like a horse saddle).
\item In high dimensions, saddle points are far more common than bad local minima: for a critical point to be a minimum,
  the curvature must be positive in \emph{every} one of millions of directions. Empirically, most local minima of large
  networks have similar, good loss values. The real enemies are plateaus and saddles, where the gradient is tiny.
\end{itemize}

\section{Momentum, RMSProp and Adam, built up step by step}

\subsection{Momentum}
Plain GD in a long narrow valley zig-zags across the steep walls while creeping along the floor. \textbf{Momentum} keeps a
running ``velocity'':
\[ \mathbf v \leftarrow \beta\mathbf v + \nabla L,\qquad \mathbf w \leftarrow \mathbf w - \eta\mathbf v,\qquad \beta\approx0.9. \]
Across the valley the gradients alternate sign and cancel in $\mathbf v$; along the floor they agree and accumulate. Like a
heavy ball rolling downhill. With a constant gradient $g$, $\mathbf v$ approaches $g/(1-\beta) = 10g$: the effective step is
up to 10 times larger along consistent directions.

\begin{example}[: momentum velocities]
$\beta = 0.9$, constant gradient 1, $v_0 = 0$: $v_1 = 1$, $v_2 = 1.9$, $v_3 = 2.71$, $v_4 = 3.44$, \dots\ $\to 10$.
\end{example}

\textbf{Nesterov momentum} evaluates the gradient at the look-ahead point $\mathbf w - \eta\beta\mathbf v$: it corrects course
before overshooting.

\subsection{AdaGrad and RMSProp: a learning rate per parameter}
Some parameters get large gradients, some small (rare features, rare words). RMSProp keeps a running average of
\emph{squared} gradients per parameter and divides by its square root:
\[ s \leftarrow \rho s + (1-\rho)g^2,\qquad w \leftarrow w - \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{s} + \epsilon}g. \]
Parameters with consistently large gradients take smaller steps; rarely updated ones take larger steps. AdaGrad uses the
\emph{sum} of all past squared gradients instead of a moving average, so its effective learning rate only ever shrinks
(good for sparse convex problems, stalls in deep learning).

\subsection{Adam = momentum + RMSProp + bias correction}
\begin{align*}
m &\leftarrow \beta_1m + (1-\beta_1)g &&\text{(average gradient: momentum)}\\
v &\leftarrow \beta_2v + (1-\beta_2)g^2 &&\text{(average squared gradient: scale)}\\
\hat m &= m/(1-\beta_1^t),\quad \hat v = v/(1-\beta_2^t) &&\text{(bias correction)}\\
w &\leftarrow w - \eta\,\hat m/(\sqrt{\hat v} + \epsilon)
\end{align*}
Defaults: $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$, $\eta = 10^{-3}$. Because $m$ and $v$ start at 0, early
averages are biased toward 0; dividing by $(1 - \beta^t)$ removes that bias.

\begin{example}[: the first Adam step]
$g = 4$, $w = 1$, $\eta = 0.01$, $t = 1$. $m = 0.4$, $v = 0.016$. $\hat m = 0.4/0.1 = 4$, $\hat v = 0.016/0.001 = 16$.
Step $= 0.01\cdot4/4 = 0.01$, so $w = 0.99$. The first step has size $\eta$ whatever the gradient's scale: Adam is
scale-invariant per parameter.
\end{example}

\textbf{AdamW} applies weight decay directly to the weights instead of through the gradient (Chapter 1); it is the
default for Transformers.

\section{Second-order methods}
Newton's method uses curvature: $\mathbf w \leftarrow \mathbf w - H^{-1}\nabla L$, where $H$ is the Hessian (matrix of second
derivatives). It jumps straight to the minimum of a quadratic and converges in a handful of steps near the optimum. But
$H$ has $d^2$ entries and inverting costs $d^3$: impossible for a million parameters. \textbf{L-BFGS} approximates
$H^{-1}$ from the last few gradients and is the default for logistic regression in scikit-learn. XGBoost uses a diagonal
second-order step per leaf (Chapter 8).

\section{Practical checklist when training is not going well}
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{X X}
\toprule
Symptom & Likely cause and fix\\
\midrule
Loss explodes / NaN & learning rate too high, exploding gradients, $\log(0)$ or division by 0: lower $\eta$, gradient
clipping, check data for inf/NaN, use numerically stable losses\\
Loss oscillates wildly & $\eta$ too high or batch too small: lower $\eta$, larger batch, momentum\\
Loss decreases very slowly & $\eta$ too low, unscaled features, saturated activations: raise $\eta$, scale features, better
init, BatchNorm, Adam\\
Loss plateaus early at a poor value & saddle/plateau, dead ReLUs, too much regularisation, bug in labels: restart with
different seed, warm restarts, cyclic LR, check data pipeline, overfit a tiny batch first\\
Train loss falls, val loss rises & overfitting: early stopping and regularisation\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\begin{practical}[: optimisation at InfoEdge scale]
Click/apply models on billions of events are trained with mini-batch SGD/Adam on sparse features, often on Spark or
GPU. The Transformers behind search and the fine-tuned LLMs in the GenAI slide (SFT, DPO, distillation) are trained with
AdamW, warmup and cosine decay. A practical rule from these systems: when you double the batch size, scale the learning
rate up (linearly for SGD, by about $\sqrt2$ for Adam) and add warmup.
\end{practical}

\section{Interview questions}

\begin{qa}{\pyq{OA: SGD vs batch gradient descent} On a dataset with 10 million rows, which is faster, stochastic or batch
gradient descent? Explain properly.}
\oneline{Per update, SGD (or mini-batch) is millions of times cheaper and in wall-clock time it reaches a good solution much
faster; batch GD takes more exact steps but each step costs a full pass over the data.}
\why
Batch GD computes the exact gradient by touching all $10^7$ rows before moving once. SGD moves after each row (or each
mini-batch of, say, 256), so in the time batch GD makes 1 step, mini-batch SGD makes about 39{,}000 steps. Early in
training, even a noisy direction is good enough to make huge progress. Near the optimum, the noise means SGD jitters rather
than converging exactly; a decaying learning rate fixes that.
Technically, to reach error $\epsilon$ on a strongly convex problem, batch GD needs $O(\log(1/\epsilon))$ iterations of cost
$O(n)$ each, and SGD needs $O(1/\epsilon)$ iterations of cost $O(1)$ each: for large $n$ and modest accuracy (which is all
generalisation needs), SGD wins.
\worked Logistic regression on 10M apply events: one epoch of mini-batch SGD with $B = 512$ gives about 19{,}500 updates
and typically gets within 0.1\% of the final log loss; batch GD would need hundreds of full passes.
\pushes \emph{``Then why ever use batch GD?''} Small datasets, when you need exact deterministic convergence, or with
second-order methods (L-BFGS) that need accurate gradients. \emph{``Why mini-batch over pure SGD?''} GPU parallelism and
lower variance per step.
\pitfall Answering ``batch GD, because it converges in fewer iterations''. Fewer iterations, but each is $n$ times more
expensive.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\pyq{INT: learning rate} What is the learning rate, how do you pick it, and how do you adjust it during
training?}
\oneline{It is the step size multiplier on the gradient; pick it with an LR range test or a log-scale search, and adjust it
with a schedule (warmup, then step/cosine decay or reduce-on-plateau).}
\why
Too small: slow; too large: oscillation or divergence. For a quadratic with curvature $h$, stability requires $\eta < 2/h$.
\textbf{Picking}: try $10^{-1}, 10^{-2}, 10^{-3}, 10^{-4}$ for a few hundred steps and compare loss curves, or run a range
test. Defaults: Adam $10^{-3}$ (from scratch), $2\times10^{-5}$ to $5\times10^{-5}$ for fine-tuning BERT; SGD with momentum
$0.1$ for ResNets with batch 256.
\textbf{Adjusting}: large $\eta$ early to make progress and escape poor regions, smaller later to settle. Warmup protects
the start; decay sharpens the end. If validation loss plateaus, cut by 10.
\pushes \emph{``What happens if you raise the batch size 8 times?''} The gradient noise drops; you can raise $\eta$ (linear
scaling rule) with a warmup.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\pyq{INT: stuck at a local optimum} Your network's loss stops improving at a poor value. What could be happening
and what do you do?}
\oneline{In deep nets it is more often a plateau, a saddle point, saturated or dead units, a learning rate problem or a data
bug than a true bad local minimum; I would debug the pipeline first, then change the optimisation (learning rate schedule,
momentum/Adam, restarts, initialisation, normalisation).}
\why
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Bug check}: can the model overfit 32 examples to near-zero loss? If not, there is a bug (labels shuffled
  relative to inputs, wrong loss, frozen layers, wrong preprocessing).
\item \textbf{Learning rate}: too low means crawling on a plateau; too high means bouncing in a valley. Try an LR finder
  and a schedule; try cyclic or warm restarts to jump out.
\item \textbf{Saturation}: sigmoid/tanh units with huge inputs have near-zero gradients; ReLUs that always output 0 are
  dead. Fix with better initialisation (He/Xavier), BatchNorm, Leaky ReLU, a lower $\eta$.
\item \textbf{Optimiser}: momentum or Adam carry through flat regions; SGD noise (smaller batches) helps escape saddles.
\item \textbf{Capacity}: if training loss itself is high, the model may simply be too small.
\item \textbf{Restarts}: different random seeds; ensembles of them.
\end{enumerate}
\pushes \emph{``Are local minima a big problem in deep learning?''} Mostly no, in large networks: most local minima are
nearly as good as the global one; saddle points and plateaus are the practical issue.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{Do three gradient-descent steps on $L(w) = (w - 3)^2$ from $w_0 = 0$ with $\eta = 0.1$. For which $\eta$ does it
converge?}
\oneline{$w = 0.6, 1.08, 1.464$; it converges for $0 < \eta < 1$.}
\why Derivative $2(w-3)$; the gap to the optimum multiplies by $(1 - 2\eta)$ each step; convergence needs $|1 - 2\eta| <
1$.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{Why does feature scaling speed up gradient descent?}
\oneline{Unscaled features create a loss surface with very different curvature in different directions; the learning rate
must suit the steepest direction, so progress along flat directions is slow and the path zig-zags; scaling makes the contours
roughly circular.}
\why For linear regression the curvature along $w_j$ is proportional to the variance of feature $j$. Salary in rupees
(variance $10^{11}$) next to experience in years (variance 10) gives a condition number of $10^{10}$. With standardisation
both have variance 1 and GD heads straight to the minimum.
\pushes \emph{``Which models do not need scaling?''} Trees and tree ensembles (they split on thresholds, invariant to
monotone transforms). Naive Bayes mostly. Everything distance- or gradient-based needs it.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{Explain momentum intuitively and mathematically.}
\oneline{Momentum keeps an exponentially weighted average of past gradients and steps along it, so consistent directions
accelerate and oscillating directions cancel, like a heavy ball rolling down a valley.}
\why $\mathbf v_t = \beta\mathbf v_{t-1} + \nabla L$; the effective step for a constant gradient approaches $\eta g/(1-\beta)$.
In a ravine the across-ravine gradient flips sign every step and averages out, while the along-ravine component adds up.
\worked See the velocity sequence $1, 1.9, 2.71, 3.44 \to 10$ for $\beta=0.9$.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{Explain Adam. Why does it need bias correction?}
\oneline{Adam keeps moving averages of the gradient (momentum) and of its square (per-parameter scale) and steps by their
ratio; because both averages start at zero they are biased toward zero early on, and dividing by $1-\beta^t$ corrects this.}
\why At $t=1$, $m_1 = (1-\beta_1)g = 0.1g$, ten times too small; $\hat m_1 = m_1/(1-0.9) = g$. Without correction the first
steps would be tiny for $m$ and, more dangerously, $v$ would be tiny too ($0.001g^2$), so $m/\sqrt v$ could be large and erratic.
\pushes \emph{``Adam or SGD?''} Adam converges faster with little tuning and is standard for Transformers and NLP; SGD with
momentum often generalises slightly better for CNNs on vision. AdamW fixes Adam's interaction with L2.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{Compute one Adam step: $g = 4$, $w = 1$, $\eta = 0.01$, $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.999$, $t = 1$.}
\oneline{$\hat m = 4$, $\hat v = 16$, step $= 0.01$, new $w = 0.99$.}
\why $m = 0.1\cdot4 = 0.4$; $v = 0.001\cdot16 = 0.016$; $\hat m = 0.4/0.1 = 4$; $\hat v = 0.016/0.001 = 16$; $\sqrt{\hat v} = 4$;
step $= 0.01\cdot4/4$.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep Why does SGD with a constant learning rate not converge exactly, and why might that be a feature rather than
a bug?}
\oneline{Its gradient is a noisy estimate, so even at the minimum it keeps taking random steps of size proportional to $\eta$;
that noise also helps escape sharp minima and saddles and biases training toward flat minima that generalise better.}
\why At the optimum the average gradient is zero but individual mini-batch gradients are not. The stationary cloud has
variance roughly proportional to $\eta\cdot\text{(gradient variance)}/B$. Sharp minima (high curvature) cannot hold a
noisy SGD iterate, so it drifts to wide basins; wide basins are less sensitive to the shift between training and test
data. To get exact convergence, decay $\eta$ (Robbins--Monro: $\sum\eta_t = \infty$, $\sum\eta_t^2 < \infty$) or average
the iterates.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep Large-batch training often generalises slightly worse. Why?}
\oneline{Larger batches mean less gradient noise and fewer updates per epoch, so training tends to settle into sharper minima
and explores less; this can be largely recovered by scaling the learning rate, warmup and longer training.}
\why Gradient noise scale is about $\eta/B$. Keeping $\eta/B$ constant (linear scaling rule) restores the noise level.
Methods like LARS/LAMB adapt per-layer learning rates for very large batches.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{What is gradient clipping? Compute the clipped gradient for $g = (3, 4)$ with norm threshold 1.}
\oneline{If the gradient's length exceeds a threshold, rescale it to that length, keeping its direction; here
$\norm{g} = 5$ so the clipped gradient is $(0.6, 0.8)$.}
\why Clipping prevents one exploding step from destroying training, standard for RNNs and Transformers. Clipping by norm
keeps the direction; clipping by value (each component to $[-c, c]$) can change it.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep Is gradient descent guaranteed to find the global minimum of logistic regression? Of a neural network?}
\oneline{For logistic regression yes (with a suitable learning rate), because the loss is convex; for a neural network no
guarantee, but in practice large networks reach minima that are nearly as good as any.}
\why Convexity means every local minimum is global and GD with $\eta < 2/L$ ($L$ = largest curvature) converges. Neural
network losses are non-convex because of symmetries (swapping two hidden units gives the same function) and
non-linearities. Over-parameterisation makes the landscape benign in practice.
\pushes \emph{``What if the logistic data are separable?''} There is no finite minimum; GD keeps growing $\norm{\mathbf w}$
(direction converges to the max-margin SVM solution, interestingly).
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{Why is Newton's method rarely used for deep learning, and where is second-order information used?}
\oneline{The Hessian of a network with $d$ parameters has $d^2$ entries and inverting it costs $d^3$, which is impossible
for millions of parameters, and it can be indefinite (saddles); quasi-Newton L-BFGS is used for small convex problems and
XGBoost uses per-leaf second-order steps.}
\why With $d = 10^8$, the Hessian would need $10^{16}$ numbers. Newton also moves toward saddle points as readily as minima.
Approximations (K-FAC, Shampoo) exist in research and large-scale training.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{How many parameter updates happen in 20 epochs with 50{,}000 examples and batch size 250?}
\oneline{$50{,}000/250 = 200$ updates per epoch, so 4{,}000 updates.}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep Loss becomes NaN after a few epochs. Debug it out loud.}
\oneline{I would check, in order: input data for NaN/inf, numerically unstable operations (log of zero, division by zero,
exp overflow), learning rate and exploding gradients, and mixed-precision overflow.}
\why
\begin{enumerate}
\item Assert no NaN/inf in inputs and labels (\code{np.isfinite}). A single bad row can poison a batch.
\item Use stable loss functions: \code{BCEWithLogitsLoss} instead of sigmoid then log; log-softmax instead of
  softmax then log. Add $\epsilon$ in denominators.
\item Log the gradient norm per step: a spike before NaN means exploding gradients: lower $\eta$, clip gradients, add
  normalisation.
\item In fp16, loss scaling may overflow; use bf16 or dynamic loss scaling.
\item Reproduce on a small batch with anomaly detection (\code{torch.autograd.set\_detect\_anomaly(True)}).
\end{enumerate}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{What are the differences between AdaGrad, RMSProp and Adam?}
\oneline{All scale steps per parameter by past squared gradients; AdaGrad sums them forever (learning rate only decays),
RMSProp uses a moving average (adapts to recent scale), Adam adds momentum and bias correction to RMSProp.}
\why AdaGrad suits sparse convex problems (rare features get large steps). In deep nets its accumulated sum grows without
bound, freezing learning. RMSProp's forgetting fixes that. Adam's momentum smooths the direction.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep Gradient boosting is called ``gradient descent in function space''. What does that mean?}
\oneline{Instead of updating a parameter vector by minus the gradient, each boosting round adds a small tree fitted to
the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the current predictions, so the predictions themselves take a gradient
step.}
\why Treat the vector of predictions $(F(x_1),\dots,F(x_n))$ as the thing to optimise. The gradient of $\sum\ell(y_i,F_i)$ with
respect to $F_i$ is $\partial\ell/\partial F_i$; for MSE, minus the gradient is the residual $y_i - F_i$. A tree fitted to these
values generalises the step to new $x$. The learning rate (shrinkage) is $\eta$. Chapter 8 develops this.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{What is early stopping, and why is it a form of regularisation?}
\oneline{Stop training when validation loss stops improving and keep the best checkpoint; because weights start small and grow
during training, stopping early limits their size, similar to an L2 penalty.}
\why For a quadratic loss with GD from zero, the number of steps $t$ and learning rate $\eta$ act like $1/\lambda$ in Ridge:
directions with large curvature are fitted early, small-curvature (noisy) directions late. Use patience (for example 5 epochs
without improvement) to avoid stopping on noise.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep Why does warmup help when training Transformers with Adam?}
\oneline{At the start, Adam's second-moment estimates are based on very few gradients and are unreliable, and the network is
in an unstable region (especially with post-LayerNorm); a small learning rate at first prevents a few huge, badly scaled steps
from wrecking the model.}
\why Early $\hat v$ can be tiny for some parameters, making $\hat m/\sqrt{\hat v}$ large; the attention softmax can saturate.
Linear warmup over the first 1--10\% of steps lets statistics accumulate. RAdam is a variant that corrects the variance of
the adaptive term automatically.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep A colleague says ``we use the closed-form solution, so we do not need to worry about optimisation''. When can
the closed form be worse than gradient descent?}
\oneline{When the matrix is ill-conditioned or singular (numerical error), when the data do not fit in memory, when $d$ is huge,
and when early-stopped GD gives useful implicit regularisation.}
\why $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ amplifies rounding error by the condition number; QR or SVD solvers are safer. With $d = 10^6$ sparse text
features, forming $X^\top X$ is impossible. For underdetermined problems, GD from zero gives the minimum-norm solution, often
desirable.
\end{qa}
