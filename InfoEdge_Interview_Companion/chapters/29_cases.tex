reported from real Round 4 interviews; the rest are built from systems InfoEdge described in its 2026--27 pre-placement talk (Resdex search, the

\begin{qa}{\pyq{Round 4, reported} Given a user's full browsing data on 99acres, design features (and a model) to predict whether they will buy a property.}
\oneline{Frame it as a time-windowed binary classification (``will this user transact or book a site visit within $N$ days''), engineer intent features along recency, frequency,
depth, consistency (price-band and locality concentration) and trend, train a gradient-boosted classifier on a time-based split, and evaluate by precision at the top of the ranked
list that sales will act on.}
\why
\textbf{1. Goal and decision.} Prioritise high-intent users for telecalling, developer leads or personalised offers. The decision is a ranking, so the top of the list matters most.
\textbf{2. Label.} ``Bought'' is often offline; use the best observable proxy: confirmed transaction (developer CRM), site visit booked, or a lead marked ``converted'' by an agent,
within 30--60 days after the prediction date. Note selection bias: outcomes are mostly observed for users who were called or who enquired.
\textbf{3. Row.} One user--date snapshot; features computed only from data before the snapshot date (no leakage).
\textbf{4. Features} (I would generate them systematically):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Recency and frequency}: days since last visit; sessions, searches and listing views in the last 1/7/30 days; returning after a long gap (a life event).
\item \emph{Depth}: time on listing pages, photo and floor-plan views, EMI calculator use, shortlists, contact-number reveals, enquiries, repeated visits to the same listing.
\item \emph{Price-band concentration} (the feature that impressed the interviewer): the share of viewed listings within $\pm$15\% of the median viewed price; also the
  coefficient of variation of viewed prices. Someone who looks mostly at 30-lakh flats with a few 10- and 60-lakh outliers is a 30-lakh buyer; the outliers are noise.
  Narrowing price spread over time signals a decision forming.
\item \emph{Location focus}: localities of the last 10--15 searches; entropy of locality distribution (low = focused); distance between viewed listings and the user's city; a
  Noida searcher will not buy in Bangalore however similar the listing.
\item \emph{Property-type focus}: BHK and type entropy; ready-to-move vs under-construction preference.
\item \emph{Stage signals}: loan-eligibility checks, legal or registration guides read, ``ready to move'' filters, visiting builder pages.
\item \emph{Profile}: stated budget vs viewed prices (mismatch means early stage), owner vs tenant, account age.
\item \emph{Market context}: inventory and price trend in the focus locality; festive season.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{5. Baseline.} Rule: ``enquired in last 7 days''. \textbf{6. Model.} LightGBM on these features (handles non-linearity, missing values, mixed scales); class weights for
the rare positives. \textbf{7. Offline evaluation.} Train on earlier months, test on later; metrics: precision@k for $k$ equal to daily call capacity, PR AUC, calibration (to estimate
expected conversions); segment by city and by new vs returning users.
\textbf{8. Online.} A/B test against the rule-based list: conversions per call; guardrails: user complaints about calls, unsubscribes. \textbf{9. Risks.} Feedback loop (only called
users get labels: reserve random calls), privacy (consent for calls), seasonality, drift after price changes.
\pushes \emph{``What if two users have identical behaviour but one is a broker?''} Add account-type and behaviour features (many cities, many enquiries per day) and possibly a separate
broker classifier, because brokers are a different business line. \emph{``How would you handle someone who searches only at night on mobile?''} Context features; but ask whether it
causally relates to intent or is just a demographic proxy.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\pyq{Round 4, reported} You are the sales head of Naukri.com. Give three things you would do to increase revenue. No clarifying questions allowed.}
\oneline{Assuming most revenue comes from recruiters paying for job postings and Resdex database access, and that recruiter value depends on finding good candidates quickly, I would (1)
grow and activate the seeker side to raise match quality, (2) increase revenue per recruiter with outcome-focused premium products and smarter pricing, and (3) open new recruiter
segments such as campus and SME hiring through partnerships, all measured by revenue per recruiter, renewal rate and time to hire.}
\why
\textbf{Framing.} Naukri is a two-sided marketplace with a flywheel: more and better seekers $\to$ more successful hires $\to$ recruiters pay and renew $\to$ more jobs $\to$ more seekers.
Revenue $=$ number of paying recruiters $\times$ revenue per recruiter $\times$ retention.
\textbf{1. Strengthen the seeker side (match quality drives willingness to pay).} Reactivate dormant profiles with personalised job alerts on the channel each seeker prefers (the
``seeker acquisition and reactivation engine'' and multi-channel touchpoints: mailers, notifications, WhatsApp); improve profile completeness with AI resume help; this increases
applies per job, the metric recruiters actually buy.
\textbf{2. Revenue per recruiter.} Outcome-based and tiered products: PremiumX-style premium discovery with explainable shortlists; AI REX-style automated sourcing and screening sold as
time-to-hire reduction; usage-based Resdex pricing for heavy users; upsell based on predicted hiring need (a model of which accounts are likely to expand).
\textbf{3. New segments and partnerships.} Campus hiring: partner with colleges by giving them a mini placement portal on Naukri where companies post openings (the candidate's
reported idea), which brings fresher supply and campus-recruiting budgets; SME self-serve hiring with low-cost packages; data products (Talent Pulse) sold to enterprises and HR consultancies.
\textbf{Measurement.} For each, an experiment with revenue per account, renewal rate, and a guardrail on seeker experience (spam complaints).
\pushes \emph{``Which one first?''} The one with the fastest, most measurable impact: reactivation of seekers via channel-optimised alerts, because it lifts applies per job for all existing
recruiters, which improves renewals.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep 99acres ML-based telecalling (from the 2026--27 slide): design the system that turns users and projects into a ranked call list of high-intent leads.}
\oneline{Three models in sequence, as on the slide: infer each user's requirement (budget, location, configuration), match users to project bundles, and rank user--bundle pairs by predicted
conversion (and value), then fill the callers' queues from the top, measuring conversions per call and learning from call outcomes.}
\why
\textbf{User requirement model.} From browsing and search history, predict the user's budget band, preferred localities and BHK (multi-output classification or simply robust statistics such
as the mode of the price band and localities weighted by recency). Output a structured requirement with confidence.
\textbf{User--project matching.} Retrieve projects that satisfy hard constraints (city, budget $\pm$ tolerance, BHK) and score soft fit (amenities, distance to work, developer
reputation); a two-tower or GBM matcher trained on past enquiries and site visits. Bundle 2--3 projects per user so one call can pitch several.
\textbf{User ranking.} Predict $P(\text{site visit or conversion}\mid\text{call})$ for each user--bundle with LightGBM; multiply by expected value (project commission) for revenue-aware
ranking; calibrate so expected conversions can be planned.
\textbf{Operations.} Capacity: $C$ callers $\times$ $k$ calls/day; assign by language and city; avoid re-calling too soon; respect consent and do-not-call lists.
\textbf{Labels and bias.} Only called users have outcomes: reserve a small random-call slice for unbiased training and evaluation; use call dispositions (no answer, not interested, visit booked).
\textbf{Evaluation.} Offline: precision@k on a later period. Online: A/B test conversions per call-hour against the current list; guardrails: complaint rate, user unsubscribes.
\textbf{Iteration.} Learn the best time to call (uplift by hour), and which users the call actually influences (uplift modelling) rather than who would convert anyway.
\pushes \emph{``Why uplift instead of conversion probability?''} Calling a user who would buy anyway wastes a call; the business value is the \emph{increase} in conversion caused by the call.
Uplift models (two-model or class-transformation approaches) need randomised call/no-call data.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep Resdex search: a recruiter types ``sr data scientist blr 5+ yrs python''. Design the search system (the slide's query intelligence $\to$ retrieval + ranking $\to$
personalisation).}
\oneline{Parse and normalise the query into structured intent with the taxonomy, retrieve candidates with hybrid sparse and dense retrieval within hard filters, rank them with a learning-to-rank
model per function segment, and personalise using the recruiter's past actions; evaluate with NDCG and recruiter actions per search.}
\why
\textbf{Query intelligence (QI).} Tokenise; map ``sr''$\to$Senior, ``blr''$\to$Bengaluru, ``5+ yrs''$\to$experience $\ge5$, ``python''$\to$skill; identify department (Data Science) and
premium/targeting needs (the slide's M1--M4: taxonomy, department, premium, targeting models). Use dictionaries, NER and the SilverSkAI taxonomy; expand synonyms (``ML''$=$``machine learning'').
\textbf{Retrieval.} From 30 lakh+ daily-active candidate pool: sparse BM25 on skills/titles (precise, handles rare skills), dense bi-encoder embeddings (semantic: ``data scientist'' $\approx$
``ML engineer''), hard filters (location, experience); merge (reciprocal rank fusion); the slide shows separate retrieval models per segment (finance, core engineering, sales, IT)
and per field (skill, title, skill+title), plus ``company boost'' and ``catch-all'' streams.
\textbf{Ranking.} LambdaMART/GBM per segment (the ``M10'' rankers) on features: skill match coverage, title similarity, experience fit, notice period, current CTC vs budget, recency of
profile update, activity (likely to respond), past recruiter engagement with similar profiles, profile completeness. Labels from recruiter actions (view $<$ download/contact $<$ shortlist),
debiased for position.
\textbf{Personalisation.} Recruiter-specific preferences (enterprise vs consultant, the slide's ``ER'' and ``Consultant'' models): preferred colleges, companies, past shortlists.
\textbf{Evaluation.} Offline NDCG@10 on held-out searches; online: contacts per search, time to first contact, recruiter retention.
\textbf{Constraints.} 16 lakh searches/day: latency budget about 100--200 ms; precompute candidate embeddings; ANN index; cache popular queries.
\textbf{Risks.} Fairness (avoid proxies for gender/age), freshness (stale profiles), gaming (keyword stuffing).
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep Design the job--CV matching engine that decides which applicants a recruiter sees first in the apply pool.}
\oneline{Score every application for fit to the job with a model trained on recruiter feedback (shortlist vs reject), combining structured matching features with text-similarity from a fine-tuned
cross-encoder, then rank the pool and explain the top reasons.}
\why
\textbf{Labels.} Recruiter actions on applications: shortlisted/contacted (positive), rejected (negative), ignored (weak negative; position-biased).
\textbf{Features.} Must-have skills coverage (via taxonomy), experience range fit, location/relocation, salary expectation vs budget, education requirements, title trajectory, notice period,
semantic similarity of JD and CV (bi-encoder cosine; cross-encoder score for the top part), application timing.
\textbf{Model.} GBM ranker or a fine-tuned cross-encoder whose score is a GBM feature; per-function models if behaviour differs.
\textbf{Evaluation.} NDCG@20 of shortlisted applicants; online: recruiter time to shortlist, shortlist rate of top-20.
\textbf{Explainability.} SHAP or rule-based reasons (``matches 8/9 required skills; 6 yrs vs 5--8 required''), mirroring PremiumX's ``AI reasons'' and ``search highlights''.
\textbf{Fairness.} Exclude protected attributes and audit proxies; monitor selection rates by group.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep Jeevansathi: recommend profiles to a user (slide: preferences $\to$ pool generation $\to$ content, activity and collaborative models $\to$ ranked profiles $\to$ filters and
personalisation).}
\oneline{Generate a candidate pool that satisfies stated preferences, score each candidate with content (profile similarity), activity (likelihood to respond) and collaborative (people like you
liked) models combined in a ranker that predicts \emph{mutual} interest, then apply filters and diversity, and evaluate by mutual acceptances.}
\why
\textbf{Two-sided}: success requires both users to accept, so model $P(\text{A likes B})\times P(\text{B likes A})$ (reciprocal recommendation), otherwise popular profiles are flooded and
everyone else ignored.
\textbf{Pool}: hard preferences from the slide's example (community, cities), relaxed slightly if the pool is small.
\textbf{Models}: content (age, education, profession, location similarity), activity (online recency, response rate), collaborative (matrix factorisation or two-tower on interest/accept
interactions).
\textbf{Ranking}: GBM over these scores and features, optimising mutual acceptance; diversity and fairness of exposure.
\textbf{Metrics}: interests sent, acceptance rate, mutual matches, conversations started; guardrails: reports/blocks.
\textbf{Trust}: fake-profile detection upstream (photo checks, behaviour).
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep Build the salary engine behind ``Salary Intelligence'' (Data Factory/TopTier slides): given title, city, experience, company, skills, show the typical salary and a range.}
\oneline{Clean and validate salary data, normalise titles and companies with the taxonomy, model log salary (or a Gamma GLM / GBM) for the centre and quantile models for the 10th--90th band,
shrink sparse company estimates toward their industry, and validate with MAPE and interval coverage by segment.}
\why
\textbf{Data}: self-reported CTCs (noisy, MNAR: high earners hide), offers, job-post salary ranges. Clean units (LPA vs monthly), remove impossible values, deduplicate.
\textbf{Normalisation}: title and company to canonical IDs (SilverSkAI); experience bands.
\textbf{Model}: GBM on log salary with features title, function, city tier, company (target encoding with shrinkage), skills (embedding or top-k binary), education, industry; quantile GBMs
for the band; enforce monotonicity in experience.
\textbf{Sparse cells}: hierarchical shrinkage (company $\to$ industry $\to$ function).
\textbf{Evaluation}: MAPE/RMSLE on held-out recent reports; 80\% interval should cover about 80\% of truths; bias checks by city and gender.
\textbf{Product}: ``you earn more than 68\% of software engineers in Delhi'' is a percentile from the predicted conditional distribution; communicate uncertainty when data are thin.
\pushes \emph{``Heteroscedasticity?''} Expected: spread grows with level; log target or Gamma GLM handles it (Chapter 4).
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep SilverSkAI taxonomy: normalise 50 lakh raw skill strings (``ML'', ``machine-learning'', ``M.L.'', ``Machine Lerning'') into about 70K canonical skills.}
\oneline{Clean strings, generate candidate canonical matches with character n-gram and embedding nearest-neighbour search, decide matches with a trained classifier using string, semantic and
co-occurrence features, route uncertain cases to human review, and keep the canonical set curated with clustering for discovering new skills.}
\why
\textbf{Steps}: (1) normalise case, punctuation, whitespace; expand abbreviations from a dictionary; (2) candidate generation: TF-IDF on character 3-grams + cosine, FastText/BERT embeddings + ANN,
edit distance; (3) features per (raw, canonical) pair: edit distance, Jaccard of tokens, embedding cosine, how often they co-occur with the same other skills in resumes, frequency;
(4) classifier (GBM) trained on human-labelled pairs; (5) thresholds: auto-map high confidence, human-review medium, new-skill candidate otherwise; (6) cluster unmapped strings to propose new
canonical skills (``LangChain'' when it appears).
\textbf{Evaluation}: precision/recall of mappings on a labelled sample, weighted by string frequency; downstream: search recall improvement.
\textbf{Pitfalls}: ambiguous strings (``Java'' island vs language; ``Spark'' Apache vs automotive) need context (the rest of the resume); hierarchy (``PyTorch'' is-a ``Deep learning
framework'').
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep Spam and fake recruiters (the panel's own example): design detection for job postings and recruiter messages.}
\oneline{Treat it as rare-event classification combining content (text of posts and messages), behaviour (posting velocity, payment, response patterns) and network signals, with recall-focused
but precision-guarded thresholds feeding automated blocks and a human review queue, evaluated with recall at fixed precision and refreshed often because adversaries adapt.}
\why
\textbf{Labels}: user reports, moderation decisions, confirmed frauds (asking candidates for money, fake companies). Noisy and delayed.
\textbf{Features}: text (TF-IDF/BERT: ``registration fee'', ``WhatsApp only'', unrealistic salary for title); recruiter account age, verification status, company domain match, posts per
hour, near-duplicate posts across accounts, apply-to-response ratio, candidate complaints, payment method.
\textbf{Model}: GBM on tabular + text model scores; anomaly detection (Isolation Forest) for new patterns.
\textbf{Metrics}: the panel's point: \emph{look at recall, not accuracy}; also precision at the review queue capacity; time to detection.
\textbf{Actions}: tiered: block (very high score, high precision), hold for review, monitor.
\textbf{Adversarial}: rotate features, monitor drift weekly, red-team.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep AI REX: how would you evaluate the screening agent that decides which candidates move forward after automated pre-screening?}
\oneline{Measure it like a classifier against human judgement and hiring outcomes: recall of genuinely suitable candidates (not losing good people), precision of those forwarded (recruiter
time), agreement with expert recruiters on a labelled sample, calibration, fairness across groups, and end-to-end time to hire in an A/B test.}
\why
\textbf{Offline}: sample conversations and answers; expert recruiters label fit/no-fit; compute precision/recall/F1, Cohen's kappa vs humans; error analysis by job type and language.
\textbf{Online}: A/B (AI REX vs standard process) on time to shortlist, interview-to-offer ratio, recruiter satisfaction, candidate drop-off and complaints.
\textbf{Guardrails}: no use of protected attributes; adverse-impact ratio monitoring; human approval for rejections in sensitive roles; audit logs; prompt-injection tests (candidate answers trying
to manipulate the agent).
\textbf{Calibration agent loop}: if forwarded candidates are too few, trigger more sourcing (as the slide notes).
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep Seeker reactivation: which dormant job seekers should receive which message on which channel (mail, notification, WhatsApp)?}
\oneline{Model, for each seeker and channel, the uplift in the probability of returning and applying caused by a message (not just the probability of returning), choose the best channel and
content per seeker under frequency caps, and test with holdout groups.}
\why Propensity models target people who would return anyway; uplift models target persuadable users. Features: last activity, past response by channel, profile freshness, market demand
for their skills, time of day. Content: personalised job recommendations. Constraints: daily caps, unsubscribe risk (guardrail), WhatsApp cost. Evaluation: randomised holdout; metrics:
reactivation rate, applies per reactivated user, unsubscribe rate.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep Shiksha: recommend colleges to a student who has filled a short form (exam score, preferred city, course, budget).}
\oneline{Filter colleges by hard eligibility (cut-offs, course, budget), score fit with a model trained on past students' applications and admissions (content features plus collaborative
signals from similar students), balance ``reach, match, safety'' options, and evaluate by applications and admissions.}
\why Cold start for new students is the norm (one form): content-based and similar-student (KNN on profile) methods dominate; popularity priors; explain recommendations (``students with your
rank were admitted here last year''). Fairness: avoid steering by socio-economic proxies.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\pyq{Round 4 follow-up, reported} After your feature list, the interviewer asks: ``What are the assumptions of linear regression, and what do you do if homoscedasticity is
violated?'' Answer as you would in the room.}
\oneline{Linearity, independent errors, constant variance, normal errors, no perfect multicollinearity and exogeneity; under heteroscedasticity coefficients stay unbiased but standard errors
are wrong, so I would transform the target (log or Box--Cox), use weighted least squares or robust standard errors, or model the variance directly with a GLM such as Gamma with a log link.}
\why Full answers are in Chapters 2 and 4; in the room, mention both the transformation the interviewer expected and the GLM the candidate proposed, and say when you would pick each.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep Content feed for job seekers (slide: insights generation agent $\to$ content manifestation agent $\to$ formats $\to$ personalised recommendation). How would you make sure the
generated insights are true and the feed is useful?}
\oneline{Generate insights from verified aggregates in the Data Factory with statistical checks (sample size, significance, period-over-period), let the LLM only phrase pre-computed facts with
citations to the numbers, review automatically for factual consistency, and rank content per user by predicted engagement and usefulness, evaluated by dwell time, saves and downstream
applies.}
\why Example insight from the slide: ``AI jobs up 20\% in Kolkata, flat in Pune and Chennai''. Risks: small-number noise (20\% of 50 jobs), hallucinated numbers, clickbait optimisation.
Guardrails: minimum counts, confidence intervals, numeric consistency checks between the generated text and source tables.
\end{qa}
