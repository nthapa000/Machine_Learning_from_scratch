\chapter{Density Estimation and Clustering: Histograms, KDE, K-Means, GMM and EM}
\companion{StatQuest: K-means clustering}{\ytid{4b5d3muPQmA}}

The syllabus line is ``Density Estimation (KDE, K-Means etc)''. These methods answer: \emph{where does the data live, and in what
groups?} They are unsupervised, so there is no label to check against, which is why interviewers probe whether you know how to choose
$K$, what the algorithms assume, and when they fail.

\section{What is density estimation?}
A \textbf{probability density} $p(x)$ describes how data are spread: high where values are common, low where rare; it integrates to 1.
\textbf{Density estimation} means estimating $p(x)$ from samples. Uses: understanding a variable's shape (bimodal salaries), anomaly
detection (low-density points are unusual), generative modelling, and as a building block (Naive Bayes, Bayes classifiers).
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Parametric}: assume a family (Gaussian) and estimate a few parameters (mean, variance). Efficient if the family is right,
  badly wrong otherwise.
\item \textbf{Non-parametric}: let the data shape the estimate: histograms, KDE, $k$-NN density.
\item \textbf{Mixtures} sit between: a weighted sum of simple components (GMM).
\end{itemize}

\section{Histograms}
Split the range into bins of width $h$; the density in a bin is $\frac{\text{count}}{n\cdot h}$ (so areas sum to 1).
\begin{example}[: histogram density]
100 points, 20 fall in a bin of width 0.5: density $= 20/(100\times0.5) = 0.4$.
\end{example}
Weaknesses: blocky (discontinuous), depends on bin width and on where bins start, scales badly to many dimensions (bins multiply).

\section{Kernel Density Estimation (KDE)}
\subsection{The idea}
Instead of counting points in fixed bins, put a small smooth ``bump'' (a \textbf{kernel}, e.g. a tiny Gaussian) on top of every data point
and add the bumps up:
\[ \hat p(x) = \frac{1}{nh}\sum_{i=1}^nK\Big(\frac{x - x_i}{h}\Big). \]
$h$ is the \textbf{bandwidth} (width of each bump). $K$ integrates to 1, so $\hat p$ does too.

\begin{example}[: KDE by hand]
Box kernel $K(u) = \frac12$ for $|u|\le1$, $h = 1$, data $\{1, 2, 4\}$. At $x = 2.5$: $|2.5 - 1|/1 = 1.5$ (out), $0.5$ (in), $1.5$ (out).
$\hat p = \frac{1}{3\cdot1}\cdot\frac12 = 0.167$.
Gaussian kernel, $h = 1$, data $\{0, 2\}$, at $x = 1$: both points are 1 away: $\hat p = \frac{1}{2}(\phi(1) + \phi(1)) = 0.242$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Bandwidth is everything}
Small $h$: spiky estimate, one bump per point, high variance (overfitting). Large $h$: over-smoothed, real modes merged, high bias. The
kernel shape matters much less. Rules of thumb: \textbf{Silverman} $h = 1.06\,\hat\sigma\,n^{-1/5}$ (assumes roughly Gaussian data; with
$\hat\sigma = 2$, $n = 32$: $h = 1.06\cdot2\cdot0.5 = 1.06$); or choose $h$ by cross-validated likelihood.

\subsection{Limits}
Prediction cost $O(n)$ per point; boundary bias (density leaks below 0 for salaries; use log scale or reflection); curse of dimensionality
(needs exponentially more data as dimension grows).

\section{K-Means clustering}
\subsection{The algorithm (Lloyd's)}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Choose $K$ and initial centroids.
\item \textbf{Assign} each point to its nearest centroid.
\item \textbf{Update} each centroid to the mean of its assigned points.
\item Repeat 2--3 until assignments stop changing.
\end{enumerate}
It minimises the \textbf{inertia} (within-cluster sum of squares) $\sum_i\norm{\mathbf x_i - \boldsymbol\mu_{c(i)}}^2$. Each step cannot increase it,
so it converges, but only to a local minimum that depends on initialisation.

\begin{example}[: K-Means by hand]
1-D points $\{1, 2, 6, 7\}$, $K = 2$, initial centroids 1 and 2.
Assign: 1$\to$c1; 2$\to$c2; 6, 7$\to$c2. Update: c1 $=1$, c2 $= 5$.
Assign: 1$\to$c1; 2 is 1 from c1 and 3 from c2$\to$c1; 6, 7$\to$c2. Update: c1 $= 1.5$, c2 $= 6.5$.
Assign again: unchanged. Inertia $= 0.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 = 1$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Initialisation: k-means++}
Pick the first centroid at random; pick each next centroid with probability proportional to the squared distance to the nearest
existing centroid. Spreads centroids out, gives provably better starts. Also run several restarts (\code{n\_init}) and keep the lowest
inertia.
\begin{example}[: k-means++ probabilities]
Remaining points at distances 1, 2, 3 from the first centroid: weights $1, 4, 9$; probabilities $1/14$, $4/14$, $9/14$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Choosing $K$}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Elbow}: plot inertia vs $K$; it always decreases; pick where improvement flattens.
\item \textbf{Silhouette}: for each point, $a$ = mean distance to its own cluster, $b$ = mean distance to the nearest other cluster;
  $s = \frac{b - a}{\max(a,b)}\in[-1,1]$. Average over points; higher is better. Example: $a = 2$, $b = 5$: $s = 0.6$.
\item \textbf{Gap statistic}: compare inertia with that expected under a structureless reference distribution.
\item \textbf{Business usefulness}: segments must be actionable.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Assumptions and failure modes}
K-Means assumes roughly spherical, similar-sized, similar-density clusters (it draws straight boundaries halfway between centroids:
a Voronoi partition). It fails on elongated, nested or very unequal clusters; it is sensitive to outliers (means) and to feature scaling
(Euclidean distance); categorical data need K-modes. Cost per iteration $O(nKd)$.

\section{Gaussian Mixture Models and EM}
\subsection{The model}
Assume each point was generated by first picking a component $k$ with probability $\pi_k$, then drawing from $\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu_k,
\Sigma_k)$. The density is
\[ p(\mathbf x) = \sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k\,\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\Sigma_k). \]
A GMM is both a \textbf{density estimator} (flexible shapes from Gaussian bumps) and a \textbf{soft clustering} method (each point gets a
probability of belonging to each component).

\subsection{EM, step by step}
We do not know which component generated each point (a hidden variable), so maximum likelihood has no closed form. EM alternates:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{E-step}: \textbf{responsibilities} $\gamma_{ik} = \frac{\pi_k\mathcal N(\mathbf x_i\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\Sigma_k)}{\sum_j\pi_j\mathcal N(\mathbf x_i\mid\boldsymbol\mu_j,\Sigma_j)}$:
  how much component $k$ ``owns'' point $i$ (Bayes' theorem).
\item \textbf{M-step}: re-estimate with responsibilities as soft counts: $N_k = \sum_i\gamma_{ik}$, $\pi_k = N_k/n$,
  $\boldsymbol\mu_k = \frac1{N_k}\sum_i\gamma_{ik}\mathbf x_i$, $\Sigma_k = \frac1{N_k}\sum_i\gamma_{ik}(\mathbf x_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf x_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k)^\top$.
\end{itemize}
Each iteration never decreases the likelihood; converges to a local optimum.

\begin{example}[: one E-step and one M-step]
1-D, $\pi = (0.5, 0.5)$, $\mu = (0, 4)$, $\sigma = 1$. Point $x = 1$: $\mathcal N(1\mid0,1) = 0.242$, $\mathcal N(1\mid4,1) = \phi(3) = 0.0044$.
$\gamma_1 = 0.242/0.2464 = 0.982$.
M-step example: points 0, 2, 4 with responsibilities 1, 0.5, 0 for component 1: $N_1 = 1.5$, $\mu_1 = (0 + 1 + 0)/1.5 = 0.667$, $\pi_1 = 0.5$.
\end{example}

\subsection{K-Means is a special case}
With equal, spherical covariances $\sigma^2I$ shrinking to 0, responsibilities become 0/1 (hard assignments to the nearest mean) and EM
becomes K-Means. So K-Means is ``hard-assignment EM for a GMM with identical spherical clusters''.

\subsection{Practical issues}
\textbf{Singularity}: a component can collapse onto one point (variance $\to0$, likelihood $\to\infty$); fix with a variance floor
(\code{reg\_covar}) or priors. \textbf{Choosing $K$}: BIC or AIC (likelihood penalised by parameter count). \textbf{Covariance types}:
full, diagonal, spherical, tied (fewer parameters). Parameters for $K = 3$, $d = 2$, full covariance: means 6, covariances $3\times3 = 9$,
weights 2: total 17.

\section{Other clustering methods worth knowing}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{DBSCAN}: density-based. A point is a \emph{core point} if at least \code{minPts} points lie within radius $\varepsilon$. Clusters
  are connected core points plus their border points; everything else is noise. Finds arbitrary shapes and outliers; no $K$ needed;
  struggles with varying densities. (HDBSCAN handles varying densities.)
\item \textbf{Hierarchical (agglomerative)}: start with every point alone; repeatedly merge the closest pair. Linkage: single (min distance;
  chaining), complete (max), average, \textbf{Ward} (merge that least increases within-cluster variance). Produces a dendrogram; cut at any
  level.
\item \textbf{Mean shift}: move each point uphill on a KDE to a mode; points ending at the same mode form a cluster.
\item \textbf{Spectral clustering}: cluster using eigenvectors of a similarity graph; handles non-convex shapes.
\end{itemize}

\begin{practical}[: clustering and densities at InfoEdge]
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Talent Pulse} cohorts and seeker segmentation (freshers in tier-2 cities looking for remote IT jobs vs mid-career sales):
  K-Means or GMM on behaviour features, then named by the business.
\item \textbf{Salary distributions} for a title are often bimodal (freshers and seniors): a GMM or KDE represents them; a single mean misleads.
\item \textbf{Anomaly detection}: low KDE/GMM density for posting behaviour flags suspicious recruiters.
\item \textbf{Taxonomy building}: clustering embeddings of 50 lakh raw skill strings to discover synonym groups (``ML'', ``machine learning'',
  ``M.L.''), the first step of SilverSkAI-style normalisation.
\end{itemize}
\end{practical}

\section{Interview questions}

\begin{qa}{Explain K-Means, its objective, and why it always converges.}
\oneline{Alternate between assigning points to the nearest centroid and moving each centroid to the mean of its points, which minimises the
within-cluster sum of squared distances; each step can only lower that objective and there are finitely many assignments, so it converges, though
only to a local minimum.}
\why Assignment step: for fixed centroids, nearest-centroid assignment minimises each point's term. Update step: for fixed assignments, the mean
minimises the sum of squared distances. Monotone decrease plus finite partitions implies termination.
\worked The $\{1,2,6,7\}$ example ends with centroids 1.5 and 6.5, inertia 1.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{How do you choose $K$ in K-Means?}
\oneline{Use the elbow of the inertia curve, the average silhouette score, the gap statistic, or stability across runs, and finally whether the
segments are actionable for the business.}
\why Inertia always falls with $K$ (with $K = n$ it is 0), so look for diminishing returns. Silhouette $s = (b - a)/\max(a,b)$ rewards tight,
well-separated clusters.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep Is K-Means a special case of a Gaussian mixture model?}
\oneline{Yes, in the limit: a GMM with equal mixing weights and identical spherical covariances $\sigma^2I$ with $\sigma\to0$ makes responsibilities
hard 0/1 assignments to the nearest mean, and EM then reduces exactly to K-Means.}
\why As $\sigma\to0$, $\gamma_{ik}\propto\exp(-\norm{\mathbf x_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k}^2/2\sigma^2)$ is dominated by the nearest mean. The M-step mean update is
then the plain cluster mean. GMM generalises K-Means with soft assignments, ellipsoidal shapes, different sizes and a likelihood for model selection.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{Explain EM for Gaussian mixtures and compute a responsibility.}
\oneline{EM alternates computing each point's probability of belonging to each component given current parameters (E-step) and re-estimating
weights, means and covariances using those probabilities as soft counts (M-step); for $x = 1$ between components at 0 and 4 with unit variance,
the responsibility of the first is 0.982.}
\why EM maximises a lower bound on the log-likelihood; the E-step makes the bound tight, the M-step maximises it. Each iteration increases (or keeps)
the likelihood.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep What does a GMM do when one component collapses onto a single point, and how do you prevent it?}
\oneline{Its variance shrinks toward zero and the likelihood goes to infinity, a degenerate solution; prevent it with a minimum variance (regularisation
added to the covariance diagonal), priors (MAP-EM), or restarting.}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{What is KDE, and how does the bandwidth affect it?}
\oneline{KDE estimates a density by placing a kernel bump on each data point and averaging them; small bandwidth gives a spiky, high-variance estimate,
large bandwidth an over-smoothed, high-bias one.}
\worked Silverman: $\hat\sigma = 2$, $n = 32$: $h\approx1.06$.
\pushes \emph{``How do you pick it properly?''} Maximise cross-validated log-likelihood of held-out points.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{Compute a box-kernel KDE at $x = 2.5$ for data $\{1,2,4\}$, $h = 1$.}
\oneline{Only the point 2 is within distance 1, so $\hat p = \frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac12 = 0.167$.}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep Salaries for ``Data Scientist'' look bimodal. How would you model the distribution, and why not report the mean?}
\oneline{Use a two-component GMM (on log salary) or a KDE; the mean falls in the valley between the fresher and senior humps and describes almost
nobody.}
\why Better still, condition on experience: the bimodality is probably two populations. Report the distribution or quantiles by experience band.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{When does K-Means fail? Give three examples and alternatives.}
\oneline{Non-spherical shapes (two moons: use DBSCAN/spectral), very different cluster sizes or densities (use GMM or HDBSCAN), and outliers or unscaled
features (scale, use K-Medoids or robust methods).}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{Explain DBSCAN and count the core points for 1-D points $0, 0.5, 1, 5$ with $\varepsilon = 1$, minPts $= 3$ (including the point itself).}
\oneline{DBSCAN grows clusters from dense core points and labels sparse points as noise; here 0 has $\{0, 0.5, 1\}$ (3: core), 0.5 has 3 (core), 1 has
$\{0, 0.5, 1\}$ (3: core), 5 has 1 (noise): three core points, one cluster and one noise point.}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{Single vs complete vs Ward linkage in hierarchical clustering.}
\oneline{Single linkage merges by the closest pair of points and tends to chain clusters together; complete linkage uses the farthest pair and gives
compact clusters; Ward merges the pair that least increases within-cluster variance and behaves like K-Means.}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{Why must features be scaled before K-Means? Which clustering methods do not need it?}
\oneline{K-Means uses Euclidean distance, so large-range features dominate; methods on a precomputed meaningful distance (e.g. cosine on normalised
embeddings) or density on a single scale avoid the issue, but almost all distance-based clustering needs scaling.}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep How do you evaluate clustering when there are no labels?}
\oneline{With internal metrics (silhouette, Davies--Bouldin, Calinski--Harabasz), stability under resampling, and above all external usefulness: do segments
differ on outcomes that matter (apply rate, churn) and can the business act on them?}
\why If some labels exist, use adjusted Rand index or NMI. In a case study, propose an A/B test of segment-specific campaigns.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{How would you use density estimation for anomaly detection?}
\oneline{Fit a density (KDE, GMM) on normal behaviour and flag points whose estimated density is below a threshold chosen for an acceptable alert rate.}
\why For recruiters: features like jobs posted per hour, distinct cities, salary range width; a GMM on log-transformed features; alerts on the lowest
0.1\% density. Isolation Forest is a non-density alternative.
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{What is the silhouette of a point with mean intra-cluster distance 2 and mean nearest-cluster distance 5?}
\oneline{$(5 - 2)/5 = 0.6$.}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{\deep Can you use K-Means for something other than clustering?}
\oneline{Yes: vector quantisation (compressing data or images to $K$ colours), creating features (distance to each centroid, cluster ID), initialising GMMs,
building inverted-file indexes for approximate nearest neighbour search (IVF in FAISS), and summarising large datasets.}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{How many free parameters does a GMM with $K = 3$, $d = 2$ and full covariances have?}
\oneline{$17$: $3\times2 = 6$ means, $3\times3 = 9$ covariance entries (3 per symmetric $2\times2$), and $3 - 1 = 2$ mixing weights.}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}{K-Means vs GMM: when would you choose each?}
\oneline{K-Means for speed and simple, round, similar-sized clusters with hard assignments; GMM when clusters are elliptical or of different sizes, when you
need soft membership probabilities or a density, and when you want a likelihood-based way (BIC) to choose $K$.}
\end{qa}
